# Example Auto Insurance: Adjusters' manual

## About this manual

This manual is an illustration made for a software demo. It is not a real claims procedure, and Example Auto Insurance is not a real company. Real claims manuals are longer and follow each state's rules.

It tells adjusters (the staff who investigate and settle claims) how to handle a claim, step by step. It doesn't say what a customer is covered for: that's in the policy booklet for their coverage level (Liability, Liability + collision, or Full). When a step depends on coverage, check the booklet first.

## First contact with the customer

The first report of a claim is called the first notice of loss (FNOL). Call the customer back within one business day of it. On the first call:

- Check the policy was active on the date of loss, and that the driver is a listed driver.
- Ask what happened, where and when, and who else was involved.
- Ask if anyone was hurt. If so, follow the steps under "Injuries" today.
- Tell the customer what happens next and when you'll call again.

Write a claim note after every call, even a short one.

## Setting the reserve

The reserve is the amount we expect the claim to cost in total. Set it within two business days of first contact, from the repair estimate if there is one, or from similar claims if there isn't.

Change the reserve as soon as you learn something that moves the cost, such as a second estimate, an injury or a total loss. Don't wait for the claim to close. A reserve that's far too low or far too high misleads the finance team. Write a note saying why you changed it.

## Deciding fault

Every claim is marked at fault, not at fault, or shared. Decide from the evidence, not from what one driver says:

- The police report, if there is one.
- Statements from both drivers and any witnesses.
- Photos of the damage and the scene, and dashcam or store camera footage.

Rear-end collisions are usually the fault of the driver behind. If the evidence is unclear, mark the claim shared and write a note saying what's missing. You can change the fault later if new evidence comes in.

## Collision claims

For a collision between two vehicles, get the other driver's name, insurer and policy number, and the license plate of their car.

Ask the customer for photos of both cars before any repair starts. Get a repair estimate from a repair shop, or send an appraiser (a staff member who inspects damage and prices the repair) if the damage looks heavy.

If the other driver was at fault, their insurer should pay. We may still pay our customer first and recover the money later: see "Getting money back from the other insurer".

## Hit-and-run claims

A hit-and-run is a collision where the other driver leaves without giving their details. Treat it as a collision claim with these extra steps:

1. The customer must report it to the police within 24 hours. Get the police report number.
2. Ask where it happened and when. Parking lots and stores often have cameras: ask the customer to request the footage quickly, as many stores delete it after a few days.
3. Ask for photos of the damage, and of any paint or parts left at the scene.
4. Check the booklet: the customer's own car is only repaired if they have collision coverage.

Mark the claim not at fault unless the evidence says otherwise.

## Theft claims

The customer must report a theft to the police first, then to us within 24 hours. Get the police report number, and ask for every key to the car.

Ask when and where the car was last seen, whether it was locked, and whether anything was left inside. Check the claim history for earlier thefts or recent claims on the same car.

If the car is found, have it inspected for damage before it's returned. If it isn't found, the booklet says when we treat it as a total loss.

## Glass and windshield claims

Ask for a photo of the damage next to a coin, so you can see its size. A small chip or crack can usually be repaired; larger damage, or damage in the driver's line of sight, means the glass is replaced.

Send the customer to an approved glass repair shop, or book a mobile repair if they can't drive safely. Check the booklet for the customer's level before telling them what they'll pay, as it differs between a repair and a replacement.

## Fire claims

Ask the customer where the fire started and what they were doing at the time. Ask for the fire department report if one was made.

Always send an appraiser to a fire claim. A fire that starts in the engine of a well-kept car is usually a fault. A fire with no clear cause, on a car with money owed or a recent change of owner, is a reason to read "Signs of fraud".

Most burned cars are a total loss.

## Vandalism claims

Vandalism is damage done on purpose by someone else, such as keyed paint, slashed tires or smashed mirrors. The customer must report it to the police: get the report number.

Ask for photos of all the damage, and ask where the car was parked. If the customer knows who did it, write their name in a note, as we may recover the cost from them.

Check the claim history: several vandalism claims on one car in a short time need a closer look.

## Weather and hail claims

After a big storm, many claims arrive at once. Check the date and place of loss against the weather records for that day.

Ask for photos of the damage. Hail dents on the roof and hood can often be fixed by paintless dent repair (pushing the dents out from behind), which is cheaper and quicker than new panels. For flood damage, ask how high the water came: water above the floor usually means a total loss.

Check the booklet first: weather damage is only covered at some levels.

## Injuries

Treat any injury as urgent. On the day you hear about it:

- Write down who was hurt, how badly, and where they were treated.
- Raise the reserve to allow for medical bills.
- Tell your team lead if anyone went to the hospital.

Ask the injured person for their medical bills and records, with their written permission. Don't give an opinion on how serious an injury is: the medical records decide that. If a lawyer contacts us, pass the claim to your team lead.

## Choosing a repair shop

The customer may use any licensed repair shop. Offer our approved shops first: they send estimates the same day, and we guarantee their work.

Compare each estimate with similar repairs at other shops. If an estimate is much higher than usual, ask the shop to explain it, or send an appraiser. Approve extra work found during the repair only after you've seen photos of it.

## Rental cars

If the customer's car can't be driven, or is in the shop for a covered repair, check the booklet for their rental car cover and how long it lasts.

Book the rental through our rental partner, so the bill comes to us. Tell the customer the last day of the rental at the start, and remind them two days before it ends. If the repair runs late because of the shop, extend the rental and write a note saying why.

## Total loss and salvage

A car is a total loss when repairing it would cost more than it's worth. Work out its actual cash value (what it was worth just before the loss) from similar cars for sale nearby, and explain the number to the customer.

Before you pay, get the title from the customer and, if they still owe money on the car, the lender's details. The car then belongs to us. It's sold for salvage (what a damaged car is worth for parts or repair), and the sale lowers the cost of the claim.

## Getting money back from the other insurer

Subrogation means getting back what we paid from the insurer of the driver who caused the loss. Start it on every claim marked not at fault or shared, as soon as we've paid.

Send the other insurer our payments, the police report and the photos. If the customer paid a deductible, we ask for it back too, and pay it to the customer when it comes in. Write a note each time the other insurer replies.

## Signs of fraud

Most claims are honest, so never accuse a customer. Look closer if you see two or more of these:

- The claim comes soon after the policy started or the coverage went up.
- The story changes between calls, or doesn't match the damage.
- The customer pushes hard for a quick cash payment.
- There's no police report where there should be one.
- Several claims on the same car, or from the same address, in a short time.

Write down what you saw, and pass the claim to your team lead before you pay.

## Writing claim notes

Notes are the record of the claim. Anyone should be able to read them and carry on where you stopped. In each note:

- Say what happened, what you did, and what happens next.
- Write facts, not opinions about the customer.
- Write the date of any promise you made, such as a call back.

Don't copy full card numbers, bank details or medical details into a note. Write "medical records received", not what they say.

## Closing a claim

Close a claim once nothing more will be paid or recovered. Every claim ends as one of these:

- **Settled:** we've paid everything we owe. Check every bill, the rental and the repair are paid.
- **Denied:** the loss isn't covered. Quote the section of the booklet that applies, and tell the customer in writing.
- **Withdrawn:** the customer chose not to go ahead, for example because the cost was under their deductible.

Write a last note saying why the claim closed.
